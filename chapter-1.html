Chapter 1: Sight Unseen

I didn't set out to find a cabin. I set out to find a place where I could live what I'd spent years studying.

At the time I was working in Wyoming. My background is sustainability and the built environment. I have a bachelor's in that field and an associate's degree in architecture. I also did two internships with the Department of Energy through the Federal Energy Management Program, known as FEMP. Those internships let me dig into geographical systems, with different layers and maps that told me a lot about where to live and the conditions I wanted to be around for the future. I had also just come back from Germany, where I finished a master's degree in renewable energy engineering and management. I spent my days thinking about how people and land fit together. At some point the question turned personal: where do I want to live that way? It's one thing to study sustainability, to look at maps and data and talk about how people should live with the land. It's another thing to actually do it. I didn't want it to stay on paper. I wanted to wake up every day inside the thing I had been studying, and see if I could really live it.

I knew what I wanted. Mountains. Water. Low humidity. A cooler climate. When I put those together on a map, the answer kept pointing to the northwest quadrant of the country. I wasn't picking a place because a friend lived there or because I had a job lined up. I was picking it because of the land itself. That was new for me. Most people move for work or for family. I was moving for mountains and water, and for a climate I believed I could live in for the long run.

Then I started ruling places out. Coastal Seattle was too expensive, even though I love the sunsets in Vancouver. Montana was too rural for me. That left northern Idaho. I did love those Vancouver sunsets, and part of me wished I could make the coast work. But I had to be honest with myself about money. And when I say Montana was too rural, I mean I wanted space and quiet, but I didn't want to be so far out that every trip to town became a whole day. Northern Idaho sat right in the middle. It had the mountains, the water and the cooler weather, without the coastal prices, and it still had towns close by. The more I looked at it, the more it felt like the answer had been there the whole time.

I went looking on Zillow, and there it was: a half-acre property for $95,000. I bought it in cash, sight unseen. That cash came from risky options trades on the stock market, trades that ended in significant losses. In 2022, the year I bought the property, I lost so much money. But at least I locked in this little piece of land that was mine. I won't pretend that year felt good. Losing money like that hurts, and it makes you question your own judgment. But when I look back, I see it differently. Most of what I made in those trades is gone. The land is still here. Whatever else happened that year, I came out of it owning something real, something I could stand on. God loves me and I believe in God, and I think He let me hold on to the one thing that mattered.

I know how that sounds. All I had to go on was Google Earth. I zoomed around the area to get a feel for it, and what I saw looked like ranch and farm country, not a city. I spent a lot of time zoomed in on that little square of land from above, trying to picture what it would be like to stand there. Buying a place you've never seen takes a certain kind of trust. Some people would call it crazy. For me, hitting buy was a leap of faith. Everything lined up: the right weather, the mountains, plenty of water in the area. I hadn't seen where it was, but I trusted that God was sending me to the right place.

About six months later, I flew out with a plane ticket and a week-long rental car. Those six months were a strange kind of waiting. I owned a piece of land I had never touched. I knew it from pictures and from Google Earth, and that was all. When I finally got on the plane, I didn't know if I was flying toward the best decision I had ever made or the biggest mistake. I only knew I had to go see it for myself.

The property had a cabin, and the cabin was run-down. It was not ready to live in. The woman who had lived there before me had clearly been through a rough living situation, and the place showed it. The grass was overgrown, and there were fruit trees, though I had no idea what kind. I'm not going to pretend it looked like the pictures in my head. It didn't. It looked tired. But I didn't feel sorry I had bought it. I felt like the place had been waiting for someone to take care of it, and now that someone was me. I don't know the full story of the woman who lived there before me, and it isn't mine to tell. I just know she had been through a hard time, and I tried to treat the place with respect because of it.

I didn't know what animals were around either. I found droppings and wondered if a bear had been through. With so many trees and so much tall grass, I couldn't tell. It turned out deer used to sleep there, and the droppings were theirs. I laugh about it now, but at the time I was careful. I was a guy who had never been there before, standing in tall grass in North Idaho, not knowing what was out there with me. Finding out it was deer made me feel better, and honestly it made me happy. The deer had been using that land as a place to rest. In a way, they were there first.

Standing there, I felt like I was in the middle of the forest. Yet the hospital in town was right next door. It felt wild and close to everything at the same time. That was exactly what I had been looking for without knowing how to say it. I wanted to feel like I was out in nature, but I didn't want to be cut off. Out there, I could have both.

What I didn't know yet was how the cabin worked. I didn't understand any of its systems. Later I found out it ran on solar power, and the batteries were in pretty bad shape. The plumbing was in pretty bad shape too. I had to discover how everything worked and fix it, and my master's degree in renewable energy engineering and management turned out to be exactly what I needed for that. I had studied these systems in classrooms and in Germany, and now here was one in front of me, falling apart. It was like the test showed up before I knew I was taking it. I didn't have a manual. I had to look at each piece, figure out what it did, and figure out why it wasn't doing it. Some days I felt smart. Some days I felt like I didn't know anything at all. But every system I figured out made the cabin feel a little more like mine.

The first real reminder of how much work lay ahead was the bathroom. The cabin couldn't be lived in yet, so when I needed one, I drove to the nearby casino. That's not exactly the picture most people have of homesteading. You think of a cozy cabin in the woods, not a guy driving to a casino to use the restroom. But that was my reality, and I didn't mind. It was humbling, and it reminded me every day how far the place had to go.

The first stay was rough. There was no heating. There were space heaters in the cabin, but I didn't know how to work them, so I bought a small portable propane heater. I could smell the propane in the room, which wasn't great. But I was still happy to be there. I bought a bottle of wine and celebrated alone. Some people might think celebrating alone sounds sad. It wasn't. I had taken a big risk, I had made it to the land, and I was sitting inside my own cabin, cold and smelling propane, but in a place that was mine. That deserved a toast, even if the only one there to hear it was me. I believe God was there too, and I was thankful.

I didn't stay at the cabin the whole week. I spent a few nights at the Holiday Inn in Spokane, paid for with credit card points, and used those days to recover. Then I'd head back and spend a few days at the cabin. I learned early that I couldn't push through everything at once. The cabin was hard on me in those first days, and a few nights away helped me come back ready to work again. I don't see that as quitting. It was pacing myself. You can't fix a place if you wear yourself out on the first week.

When I got dirty from the work, I went down to the river to wash off. There was something simple and good about that. No hot water, no bathroom, just me and the river. It's one of those memories that sounds rough when you say it out loud, but I remember it fondly. It made me feel connected to the place, like the land was taking care of me while I was trying to take care of it.

Slowly I started making the place livable. I moved some furniture around. The bed that was there was a twin, so I looked on Facebook Marketplace, found a full-size bed, and bought a new mattress to go with it. I also bought plywood to cover some of the exposed insulation in the ceiling, which made the room more comfortable. None of those were big changes on their own. A bigger bed, a new mattress, some plywood on the ceiling. But each one made the room feel a little more like somewhere a person could live, instead of somewhere a person was just getting by. That's how it went from the start: one small thing at a time.

That first visit was really about discovering the place and getting comfortable living this far from everyone. Most of my family is in Colombia. My immediate family, like my sister, is in Florida, which is still a long way from Bonners Ferry. Bonners Ferry is only about 30 minutes from the southern border of Canada, so I felt pretty far from everything. I felt like a complete stranger, detached from it all, very far from everyone, just by myself. I won't lie and say that didn't get to me. There were moments when the quiet was heavy. No one around to talk to, no one who knew me. But even then, I didn't feel completely alone. I believe in God, and I believe He brought me there for a reason. On the hard nights, that was what I held on to.

But I call this my home. It took time before that word felt true. At first it was just a piece of land and a run-down cabin. But somewhere along the way, it became home, and it still is.

That was my first week in Bonners Ferry.

It was later, when my mom came to visit, that I found out how little I knew about the trees. She walked off and came back with a plum. I had no idea where she got it. Everything was so overgrown that I couldn't see what was growing out there. There was a plum tree at the bottom of the property that I didn't even know about. I remember being amazed. I owned this land and I didn't even know what was growing on it. My mom had been there a short time and had already found something I had missed. It made me laugh, and it also made me realize how much the land still had to show me.

There were more trees than the plum. There are cherry trees now in front of the house that used to be the cabin. Behind the house are crab apple trees. They make tiny apples that taste just like regular apples. I didn't know at first that they were edible, and I found out later that they are. Every season since, the land has shown me something new. Flowers come up in the spring that I didn't plant. Trees I walked past without a second thought turn out to give fruit. I think about that a lot. I bought this place sight unseen, and it keeps giving me things I never knew were there. To me that feels like a gift from God, a reminder that the leap was worth it.
